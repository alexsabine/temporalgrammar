How Far Does It Go?

From the Planck length to the edge of what can be seen, in one zoom. Sixty-two powers of ten, and you are near the middle.

 

A person

seen

Everything that can be seen, drawn as a flat slice with you at the centre. Light has had 13.8 billion years to travel. Space has stretched while it travelled, so the places it left are now 46 billion light-years away. That distance is the particle horizon. This page computes it by adding up the expansion step by step with the Friedmann equation.

Farther out is also earlier. The rings mark when the light set out. The bright rim is the wall of light, and just behind it is the beginning.

Nothing beyond this line can be seen. No signal from farther away has had time to arrive. What is drawn outside the circle is imagination: the simplest guess, more of the same. The first 10⁻³² seconds are imagined too. The standard account, inflation, says the largest patterns in this picture began as quantum ripples far smaller than a proton (Guth 1981; Mukhanov & Chibisov 1981). It fits the measurements well. It has not been proven.

DoSwitch between the two measured expansion rates and watch the age and the horizon change.

The oldest light there is. For its first 370,000 years the universe was a glowing fog of loose electrons and nuclei, and light could not get through it. When it had cooled to about 3,000 K, atoms formed, the fog cleared, and the light flew free. It now arrives as microwaves from every direction, at 2.7255 K (Fixsen 2009).

The map is the whole sky. The mottling stands for places a few hundred-thousandths hotter or cooler than average. From the real map the Planck satellite found an age of 13.797 ± 0.023 billion years, an expansion rate H₀ of 67.4 ± 0.5 km/s/Mpc, 31.5% matter and 68.5% dark energy (Planck Collaboration 2020).

A disagreement. Measuring the expansion directly, from pulsating and exploding stars, gives H₀ = 73.0 ± 1.0 (Riess et al. 2022). The two differ by five times their uncertainty. Nobody yet knows why.

DoRead the chart below. The spots are strongest at about one degree across: the distance sound had travelled in the fog.

The farthest galaxies seen. The expansion has stretched their light so much that it arrives as infrared, which is why it took the James Webb Space Telescope to find them. The stretch is the redshift, z. Light at z = 14 has been stretched 15 times.

The record, as of November 2026, is MoM-z14 at z = 14.44 ± 0.02 (Naidu et al. 2025). We see it as it was about 280 million years after the beginning. Before it came JADES-GS-z14-0 at z = 14.32 (Carniani et al. 2024). Candidates beyond z = 15 have been proposed. Two bright ones turned out to be small cool stars in our own galaxy (Bradač et al. 2026), and none has been confirmed (Fontana et al. 2026).

Instrument. A mirror 6.5 m across. It separates details 1.22 λ/D apart.

DoMove the redshift and read how old the light is.

On the largest scales galaxies are not spread evenly. They lie along filaments and sheets around nearly empty voids, like the walls of a sponge. Each dot stands for a galaxy. The fans are the parts of the sky a survey can reach, either side of the Milky Way’s own disc.

Instrument. A redshift survey measures the stretch of each galaxy’s light and turns it into a distance. The Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument has done so for more than 14 million galaxies and quasars in its second data release (DESI Collaboration 2025). Its results fit the standard model, and fit a little better if dark energy is slowly weakening: a preference of 2.8 to 4.2 times the uncertainty, depending on which supernova data are added. That is a hint, not a discovery.

DoMove the redshift. Each ring is a distance and also a time.

Take away the expansion and galaxies are left drifting, each pulled by the matter around it. Follow the drift and the paths gather like streams in a river basin. Our basin is Laniakea, Hawaiian for “immense heaven”: about 160 megaparsecs across, with the mass of about 10¹⁷ Suns (Tully et al. 2014). The orange line is its edge. Outside it, galaxies drift towards somewhere else.

Instrument. No telescope sees these paths. They are worked out from the distances and speeds of thousands of galaxies.

Our own small group: the Milky Way, Andromeda 2.5 million light-years away, the smaller Triangulum galaxy, and several dozen dwarf galaxies (McConnachie 2012). Andromeda is the farthest thing you can see with your own eyes.

The light arriving tonight from Andromeda left before there were people.

The Milky Way: a disc of 100 to 400 billion stars about 100,000 light-years across, with a bar at its centre and arms winding out. The Sun is in a minor arm, about 27,000 light-years from the centre (Bland-Hawthorn & Gerhard 2016).

At the centre is a black hole of 4.3 million Suns, Sagittarius A*, weighed from the orbits of stars around it (GRAVITY Collaboration 2022). In 2022 the Event Horizon Telescope, radio dishes across the whole Earth working as one, made an image of the glowing ring around its shadow: 51.8 ± 2.3 millionths of an arcsecond across.

Nobody has seen this view. We are inside the disc. The picture follows the positions of nearly two billion stars measured by the Gaia satellite (Gaia Collaboration 2023) and radio and infrared maps.

The Sun’s neighbours. Each is placed by its parallax: as the Earth goes round the Sun, a nearby star seems to shift against the distant ones. A shift of one arcsecond means one parsec, 3.26 light-years.

The nearest, Proxima Centauri, shifts by 0.768 arcseconds: 4.2465 light-years (Gaia Collaboration 2021). Most of the neighbours are small red stars, too faint to see without a telescope.

Instrument. The Gaia satellite measured such shifts, to a few hundred-thousandths of an arcsecond, for nearly two billion stars.

Comets fall towards the Sun from every direction, on orbits tens of thousands of times wider than the Earth’s. They must come from somewhere. Oort (1950) proposed a vast round cloud of icy bodies. It probably reaches a quarter of the way to the nearest star.

Nobody has seen the Oort cloud. Its members are too small and too faint. It is inferred, and every dot here is placed by rule.

The Sun blows a thin wind of charged particles in all directions. Far beyond the planets the wind slows suddenly, at the termination shock. Farther still it is stopped by the gas between the stars. That boundary is the heliopause.

Two spacecraft have crossed it: Voyager 1 in August 2012 at 121.6 AU, and Voyager 2 in November 2018 at 119 AU (Gurnett et al. 2013; Stone et al. 2019). On 18 November 2026 Voyager 1 passed one light-day from the Earth. A radio message now takes more than 24 hours to reach it.

Instrument. The spacecraft themselves, which measure the gas they fly through. The shape drawn is a simple model (Parker 1961). The real shape, above all the tail, is not known.

DoSend a flash of light from the Sun.

Eight planets on nearly circular paths. The distance from the Earth to the Sun, 149,597,870.7 km, is the astronomical unit. Neptune is 30 times farther out.

Kepler’s third law ties the size of an orbit to its period: the square of the year goes as the cube of the distance.

DoSend a flash of light from the Sun. It reaches the Earth in 8.3 minutes and Neptune in 4.2 hours. On this scale light is slow.

The Earth and the Moon, to scale. Most drawings bring them closer. Thirty Earths would fit in the gap.

Instrument. Apollo astronauts left mirrors on the Moon. Observatories fire laser pulses at them and time the echo, which gives the distance to within millimetres.

DoSend a flash of light. It is drawn at its true speed.

The Earth from about a million miles away, where a camera on the DSCOVR satellite photographs the whole sunlit face several times a day. Its mean radius is 6,371 km.

Everything further in is somewhere on this disc.

A city at night from a few hundred kilometres up. Street lights trace the roads. The river and the parks stay dark.

Instrument. A camera in orbit. The finest detail it can separate is 1.22 λ h / D, for a lens of width D at a height h.

You are here. A person is about 1.7 m tall. That is near the middle of everything: 35 powers of ten above the smallest length that means anything in physics, and 27 below the widest distance that can be seen.

This page is one zoom between those two ends. Each stop is drawn in the style of the instrument that sees it. Where no instrument can, the picture is marked imagined.

One potassium ion from the channel, much enlarged. A nucleus of 19 protons and 20 neutrons sits at the centre with 18 electrons around it.

The electrons are not little moons. Quantum mechanics gives only the chance of finding one at each place, and that chance is what is drawn: a cloud in three shells. The natural size of an atom is the Bohr radius, 52.9 pm, fixed by the electron’s mass and charge and by Planck’s constant.

Keep going in. The cloud is nearly all of the atom’s size and almost none of its mass.

Instrument. None makes this picture; it is computed. The nodes in a hydrogen atom’s electron cloud have been recorded directly (Stodolna et al. 2013).

The nucleus: 39 particles in a ball 8 femtometres across, holding more than 99.9% of the atom’s mass.

Rutherford (1911) found it by firing alpha particles at gold foil. Most went straight through. A few bounced back, and only a tiny, heavy, charged centre could do that. Later, electrons scattered from nuclei gave their sizes (Hofstadter 1956). The radius grows as the cube root of the number of particles, as it would for drops of one liquid.

There is no image. From here down nothing is seen by forming a picture. Things are found by throwing particles at them and counting where they go.

DoRaise the alpha’s energy until it reaches the nucleus.

One proton. Its charge is spread over a radius of 0.84 fm. For some years two kinds of experiment disagreed about that by 4%. Ordinary hydrogen and electron scattering gave 0.88. Hydrogen made with a muon in place of the electron gave 0.84 (Pohl et al. 2010; Antognini et al. 2013). Newer measurements with electrons agree with the smaller value (Bezginov et al. 2019; Xiong et al. 2019), and the recommended figure is now 0.84075 ± 0.00064 fm (CODATA 2022).

Inside are three quarks, held by a field of gluons. They were found by scattering electrons hard enough to strike something small inside (Bloom et al. 1969; Breidenbach et al. 1969).

Instrument. A probe sees detail down to about its own wavelength, λ = hc/E. More energy, finer detail.

DoChange the probe’s energy.

The finest look anyone has taken. The Large Hadron Collider brings protons together at 13.6 TeV, which is a wavelength of 9 × 10⁻²⁰ m: about a ten-thousandth of a proton.

What comes out is not a picture of the small thing. It is a spray of particles in a detector the size of a building. The sprays match what is expected if quarks and electrons are points. If they had parts the pattern would change, and it has not, up to energy scales of 12.8 to 35.8 TeV depending on the test (CMS Collaboration 2018; ATLAS Collaboration 2020). A direct limit puts the radius of a quark below 0.43 × 10⁻¹⁸ m (ZEUS Collaboration 2016).

Nothing below this line has been seen. The line is drawn at ħc/Λ for the highest scale tested: 5.5 × 10⁻²¹ m. Quarks and electrons have no measured size, only upper limits.

DoCollide again.

Below the line there are no measurements at all. From here to the Planck length is about 15 powers of ten, as far as from a person to a proton. The zeptometre (10⁻²¹ m) and the yoctometre (10⁻²⁴ m) are in here.

It may be empty: the known particles and forces, unchanged all the way down. Physicists call that the desert. Or it may hold things nobody has thought of.

One hint comes from the other end of this page. If inflation happened, the ripples now spread across the whole sky began as quantum ripples somewhere in this range. The largest things would carry the print of the smallest.

Imagined. The picture is a shimmer that looks the same however far you zoom. It means only that nobody knows.

Put together three constants, for gravity, for quantum theory and for light, and one length comes out: the Planck length, √(ħG/c³) = 1.616 × 10⁻³⁵ m. Here a particle’s quantum fuzziness and its own gravity become the same size, and neither theory can be used alone.

No theory of this regime has been tested. Three candidate pictures:

  • Foam. Space itself fluctuating, smooth only from far off (Wheeler 1955).
  • Strings. Particles as tiny vibrating strings (Green & Schwarz 1984). Their size is not known; they are drawn at this scale for the picture.
  • A network. Space woven from loops, with a smallest possible area (Rovelli & Smolin 1995).

One observation bears on it. Light of different energies from distant explosions arrives together, which rules out the simplest kinds of graininess (Abdo et al. 2009; Cao et al. 2024).

Imagined. None of the three is confirmed, and they are not the only ideas.

Things to try

Six small experiments

  1. Find the two lines. Predict which is further from you in powers of ten: the smallest thing ever tested, or the edge of what can be seen.
  2. Watch light be slow. Send a flash from the Earth to the Moon and back. It is drawn at its true speed. Then go to the Voyagers and send one from the Sun.
  3. Look back to the first galaxies. Set the redshift of the farthest galaxy known. Predict how much of the universe’s age had passed when its light set out. Then change the expansion rate.
  4. Touch a nucleus. At 5 MeV an alpha particle turns back well outside the nucleus. Predict the energy at which it reaches it.
  5. Look harder at a proton. A gentle probe sees one blurred charge. A hard one finds three grains, and then more.
  6. Find the Planck mass. Every mass has a quantum length and a gravitational one. For a proton the quantum one is larger by 39 powers of ten. Predict the mass at which the two are equal.

The mathematics

What the page computes

Each equation here is run by the page, and the line under it shows the numbers in force now. Constants are the CODATA 2022 values; cosmological parameters are Planck 2018.

1. The zoom

The slider is a number z from 0 to 1. Equal steps of z multiply the width w of the frame by equal factors, and the sum is done in logarithms so that nothing is lost between 10²⁷ and 10⁻³⁵.

Frame width on a logarithmic scale ln⁡w=ln⁡w0+z(ln⁡w1−ln⁡w0)

2. Seeing small things

Whatever is used to look, light or electrons or protons, it behaves as a wave, and a wave cannot show detail much finer than its own length. Here h is Planck’s constant, c the speed of light and E the energy of the probe. An energy scale Λ that an experiment has ruled out corresponds to a length ħc/Λ.

Wavelength of a probe of energy E λ=hcE

To hold a proton of energy E on a circle takes a magnet of field B and a ring of radius ρ. The line below checks the formula against the LHC and then asks what the Planck energy would need. It ignores the energy the protons would radiate away, which makes the real answer worse.

Bending radius in a magnetic field ρ=EecB

3. Atoms and nuclei

The Bohr radius sets the size of atoms. ε₀ is the electric constant, ħ is h/2π, and m and e are the electron’s mass and charge.

The Bohr radius a0=4πε0ħ2me2

A nucleus of A particles has a radius that grows as the cube root of A. An alpha particle of energy E aimed straight at a nucleus of charge Ze stops at a distance d.

Nuclear radius, and Rutherford’s closest approach R=r0A1/3,d=2Ze24πε0E

4. The Planck scale

Three constants, G for gravity, ħ for quantum theory and c for light, combine into one length, one mass and one time.

Planck units ℓP=ħGc3,mP=ħcG,tP=ħGc5

Every mass m has two lengths. Its Compton wavelength is the size below which quantum theory will not let it be pinned down. Its Schwarzschild radius is the size within which it would be a black hole. For anything ordinary the first is vastly larger. They meet near the Planck mass, and there the two theories have to be used together. Nobody knows how.

Compton wavelength and Schwarzschild radius λC=hmc,rs=2Gmc2

5. The expanding universe

The Friedmann equation gives the rate of expansion H when the universe was a times its present size. H₀ is the rate today. The Ω are the shares of radiation, matter and dark energy today. This page uses a flat universe with a constant dark energy, three kinds of neutrino treated as massless, and the Planck 2018 numbers.

The Friedmann equation, and two integrals of it H2=H02[Ωra−4+Ωma−3+ΩΛ] t0=∫01daaH,Dhorizon=c∫01daa2H

Light that arrives with redshift z left when a = 1/(1 + z). The same two integrals, taken from that a to 1, give how long ago it left and how far away its source is now.

Looking out is looking back tback(z)=∫1/(1+z)1daaH,D(z)=c∫1/(1+z)1daa2H

The fog cleared when hydrogen atoms could hold together. The Saha equation gives the share x of hydrogen still ionised at temperature T, where n is the number of hydrogen nuclei in a cubic metre and 13.6 eV is the energy that binds the atom. It gives the right epoch. The real clearing lagged a little behind it (Peebles 1968). The same line checks the page’s sound horizon against Planck’s.

The Saha equation for hydrogen x21−x=1n(mkT2πħ2)3/2e−13.6 eV/kT

A black hole of mass M at a distance D casts a shadow of a definite angular width if it does not spin. The ring that was photographed is not exactly the shadow, so the agreement below is rough by nature.

The shadow of a black hole θ=227GMc2D

6. Where the two ends meet

If inflation happened, quantum ripples were stretched to become the pattern in the microwave sky and the seeds of the cosmic web. The strength As of that pattern is measured. The strength of the matching ripples in space itself, r times as large, has not been found: r is below 0.036 (BICEP/Keck Collaboration 2021). Together they put an upper limit on the expansion rate H during inflation, and so a lower limit on the size c/H of the ripples when they formed. MPl is the Planck mass divided by √(8π). This is the standard account. It is well supported, and it is not proven.

A reading, and seven checks

What CRR can and cannot say here

This site is where Coherence, Rupture, Regeneration is worked on. CRR is Alexander Sabine’s proposal for a grammar of change in time. It is an interpretive framework under test. It is not established physics, and none of the scientists cited on this page proposed it or endorses it. Its three equations, as the programme states them:

Coherence: the past accumulates, since the last cut C(t)=1σ∫tntL(τ)dτ,L(τ)=g(θτ)|θ˙τ|
Rupture: the first arrival at the opposite state δ(t−tn+1),tn+1=inf{t>tn:C*Ω=1}
Regeneration: the future is built from weighted memory mn+1=∑j=−1nwjΦj∑j=−1nwj,wj=qn−j·eSjΩ

θ is the system’s state on a circle of probability distributions, g is the Fisher information (how distinguishable neighbouring states are), and L is the Fisher–Rao speed. σ is a resolution, calibrated separately: the smallest change the system can tell apart. So C is accumulated arc length since the last rupture tn, in units of σ: every step taken, including steps back. C* is the geodesic distance from where the occasion began, and Ω = 1/(the manifold’s diameter) in the same units, so the cut comes the first time the system reaches the state opposite to where it began. Any wandering on the way is surplus, S = C − C* ≥ 0. After the cut, what comes next is a memory of what each earlier occasion settled (Φ), weighted by recency q and by salience eSΩ.

The reading. A zoom is not a process in time, so CRR says nothing about it directly. What the page does show is a ladder of descriptions. Each holds over a range of scales and then gives way. Images give way to scattering below the atom. Measurement gives way to imagination below 10⁻²⁰ m. At the Planck length quantum theory and gravity each stop being usable alone. And because looking out is looking back, the outward half of the page is a history: a hot fog that held for 370,000 years, cleared in a short time, and left a pattern from which everything later was built. In CRR’s words that is coherence, a rupture, and regeneration from what the past had settled. It is a way of reading the physics. It adds no number to it.

The checks. Each was written down, with what would count as passing, before the model was first run. Results are given as they came out. The bets and the outputs are kept in the site’s source under src/how-far/.

QuestionResultWhat it shows
1. Is the distance between the two states of a yes-or-no system π, so that Ω = 1/π?PassYes. This is long-established mathematics of probability (the Fisher–Rao distance), not a finding of CRR. CRR takes its Ω from it.
2. Can the clearing of the fog be read as one CRR occasion, and does it reach the cut?InconclusiveTake one hydrogen atom, ionised or not, with the chance given by the Saha equation. The CRR count reaches one half exactly where hydrogen is half ionised, as it must. It never reaches the cut at 1: a little hydrogen stays ionised for ever, and the count stops just short. The path never turns back, so the surplus is zero. All of this is the Saha equation in other words. It predicts nothing new.
3. Are the stops on this page spaced with the programme’s candidate spread, 1/2π = 0.159?FailNo. The spread is several times larger. The stops were chosen by the page’s author, so a pass would have shown nothing about nature either.
4. The share of matter in the universe today is close to 1/π. Is that CRR’s Ω?InconclusivePlanck’s value is within its uncertainty of 1/π. DESI’s is not. The share of matter changes with time and passes through 1/π only at about the present, so this is a coincidence of the epoch we live in. The programme does not claim it, and this page does not either.
5. The two lengths of a mass cross at √π Planck masses. Is that π a sign of CRR?FailNo. The π comes from writing the wavelength with h. Written with ħ, as is just as usual, the crossing is at 1/√2 Planck masses.
6. Elsewhere this site says dark energy’s density peaks where its pressure ratio w crosses −1 (Cosmic coherence). Does it, for DESI’s numbers?Pass, as an identityYes, exactly. But it does so for any dark energy whatever: the density of any fluid stops rising at the moment w passes −1, by the conservation of energy. The same holds for an unrelated model tried here. So this is not evidence for CRR. DESI’s hint that w changes at all is 2.8 to 4.2 times its uncertainty, and is not established.
7. Do the turning points of cosmic history fall at intervals CRR would expect?Not testableThere are three: matter overtakes radiation, the fog clears, the expansion begins to speed up. Three events give two intervals. No statement about their spread can be tested with two numbers.

In sum. Two checks pass, and both are mathematics that does not depend on CRR. Two fail. Three cannot be decided. Nothing on this page is evidence for CRR. What CRR offers here is a vocabulary for the places where one description ends and another begins, and the page keeps that vocabulary apart from the physics.

Where this comes from

What is known

The constants are the CODATA 2022 recommended values (Mohr et al. 2025). The astronomical unit, the parsec, the light-year and the nominal sizes of the Earth and Sun are those defined by the International Astronomical Union. The Earth’s mean radius is that of the reference ellipsoid.

The age, expansion rate, contents and last-scattering redshift of the universe are from Planck 2018. The page integrates the Friedmann equation with them and finds an age of 13.80 billion years (Planck: 13.797) and a horizon of 46.1 billion light-years. The sound horizon and its angle come out within 0.2% of Planck’s.

Time-sensitive figures were checked on 25 November 2026. Voyager distances are from JPL Horizons for that day and are extrapolated to the day you open the page. The farthest confirmed galaxy was MoM-z14 (z = 14.44), with no confirmed galaxy beyond it. The latest DESI cosmology was from its second data release. The proton radius was the CODATA 2022 value. Later results may have changed any of these.

The seven levels inside the brain were built and checked earlier for the Active Inference Institute; their sources are listed separately below.

What is left out

Every picture is generated by the page. None is a photograph or data. The Earth’s coastlines are rough. The city is invented. The galaxy is a drawing guided by surveys. The Laniakea flows come from a toy with a few masses. The cosmic web follows a rule, not a survey. The microwave sky is random, drawn from a smooth curve shaped like the measured spectrum. The collision is invented.

The pictures are joined by cross-fades. No instrument follows one thing through all the scales, and several pictures are views of the sky, not slices of space.

The cosmology is the simplest that fits: flat, with constant dark energy and massless neutrinos. If dark energy is changing, as DESI hints, the distances shift by a per cent or so. The Saha equation is used for the clearing of the fog, where the full calculation follows the atoms level by level.

The Planck-scale pictures and the “desert” are imagination, as marked. Many ideas are not shown: extra dimensions, supersymmetry, causal sets, asymptotic safety and others. Whether space has any structure at that scale is not known.

Much is missing between the stops: molecules other than one channel, cells other than neurons, the Earth’s interior, the Sun, star clusters, galaxy clusters.
