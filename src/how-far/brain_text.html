A human brain holds about 86 billion neurons (Azevedo et al. 2009). Four in every five of them are small cells packed into the cerebellum, the striped lobe at the bottom of the picture. The folded cerebral cortex, most of the brain by weight, holds about one in five.

Instrument. Magnetic resonance imaging sees inside a living head. It divides it into boxes called voxels, usually about 1 mm across, and reports one number for each.

A patch of cortex a few millimetres wide, drawn as a microscope shows tissue in which a few cells glow. From the surface down it has six layers, I to VI, each with its own kinds of cell.

One pyramidal neuron, filled with dye. Its dendrites are about four millimetres of fine cable, and they receive thousands of inputs. A signal that arrives far out on a branch leaks away as it travels to the cell body.

A branch is more than a wire. When enough inputs arrive together, receptors that were blocked open up and the branch produces a dendritic spike (Schiller et al. 2000). The cell then behaves like a small network of its own (Poirazi, Brannon & Mel 2003).

The same tissue under an electron microscope, which sees every membrane. Nothing is empty: the space between cell bodies is packed with fine branches. Colouring each one separately, section after section, gives a wiring diagram, a connectome.

The largest piece of human brain mapped this way is H01: one cubic millimetre of temporal cortex, cut into 5,019 slices, with about 57,000 cells and 150 million synapses (Shapson-Coe et al. 2024). The matching piece of mouse visual cortex has more than 200,000 cells and about 524 million synapses (MICrONS Consortium 2025). The only adult brain mapped whole is a fruit fly’s: 139,255 neurons (Dorkenwald et al. 2024).

One synapse. Above, the swollen end of an axon, full of vesicles: bubbles about 40 nm across that hold transmitter. Below, a spine on the dendrite. Between them is a cleft about 20 nm wide. The dark band under it is the postsynaptic density, where the receptors sit.

Release is a game of chance. When an impulse arrives, each waiting vesicle is released, or not, with some probability. Responses therefore come in whole steps, and sometimes nothing is released at all (del Castillo & Katz 1954).

A membrane is two sheets of fat molecules, about 4 nm thick, crowded with proteins. An average terminal holds about 300,000 protein molecules: more than 20,000 copies of those that release vesicles, about 150 of those for one of the recycling steps (Wilhelm et al. 2014).

The marked protein is an ion channel: a pore that is either open or shut. Neher & Sakmann (1976) were the first to record the current through one. In the model here each opening passes about a picoamp.

DoWatch one channel flicker. Then add channels. A thousand of them make the smooth current that Hodgkin & Huxley (1952) wrote equations for.

The narrowest part of a potassium channel: the selectivity filter, 12 Å long. Rings of oxygen atoms hold potassium ions in single file, with water between them (Doyle et al. 1998).

This is as far as any microscope has seen. In 2020 electron microscopes resolved the separate atoms of a protein for the first time: 1.22 Å for apoferritin, and 1.7 Å for a human GABA-A receptor (Nakane et al. 2020; Yip et al. 2020). The proteins were purified and frozen. Below this, nothing is seen by forming an image.
