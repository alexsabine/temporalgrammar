    The carbon layer

    Breathing in, breathing out

    Green things take carbon dioxide out of the air when they are lit and wet, and give some back all the time. The water gives it off too. It drained through soil on the way here and picked up more CO₂ than air holds, and it lets that go where it meets the sky. A small stream is a chimney for the hillside above it.

    These are estimates for the frame of the photograph, taken as about m² of ground. They come from published rates for moss carpets and headwater streams. Nothing here was measured on the day.

    How these numbers were worked out
    • Size of the frame. No ruler was in the shot. The saxifrage leaves are taken as 3 cm across, which makes the frame about 57 by 76 cm.
    • Living cover. About a third of the frame, counted from the colour of the photograph. Water covers a little under a fifth.
    • Moss and plants. Net uptake of 2 micromoles of CO₂ per square metre per second in good light and 0.5 given off at night. Yearly growth of 100 g of carbon per square metre, a middling figure for a wet moss carpet.
    • Water. 3 micromoles per square metre per second given off, at the low end of what headwater streams average (Raymond and others, Nature, 2013).
    • Rock. Silicate weathering taking up about 5 g of CO₂ per square metre a year.
    • Making the page. Roughly three million tokens read and 150 thousand written by the model in one session. Public estimates of data-centre energy per query (Google, 2025; Epoch AI, 2025) put that between 0.1 and 1 kWh. The ledger uses 0.3.
    • Carbon per unit of electricity. 470 g of CO₂ per kWh, close to the world average for 2024 (Ember). Your own grid may be far cleaner or dirtier.
    • Your visit. Sending the page to you once costs less than a hundredth of a watt-hour.

    Plainly

    What is real and what is not

    The picture is the photograph itself. Nothing in it was redrawn. A small program bends it a little, many times a second: the water slides and catches the light, the saxifrage leaves stir, and the brightness drifts as if branches were moving overhead. That movement is invented. It is the kind of thing that happens there, and none of it was recorded.

    The photograph arrived without its location, so the living things are named as far as a picture alone allows. The moss is a cushion moss of some kind. The braided sprays are probably a spikemoss. The round-leaved plant is a saxifrage: in the Alps or Pyrenees it would most likely be Saxifraga rotundifolia, on Madeira S. maderensis, on the Pacific coast of North America S. mertensiana. Knowing where it was taken would settle it.

    The small drawings in the notes show what a hand lens or microscope would find. They are drawings, and are marked as such.
