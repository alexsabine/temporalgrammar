    The carbon layer

    Breathing in, breathing out

    Green things take carbon dioxide out of the air when they are lit and wet, and give some back all the time. The water gives it off too. It drained through soil on the way here and picked up more CO₂ than air holds, and it lets that go where it meets the sky. A small stream is a chimney for the hillside above it.

    These are estimates for the frame of the photograph, taken as about m² of ground. They come from published rates for moss carpets and headwater streams. Nothing here was measured on the day.

    And the living world is large

    The patch in the photograph is one very small piece of something enormous.

    • 3 trillion treesstand on Earth. The Milky Way has 100 to 400 billion stars, so there are roughly ten trees for every star, and nearly 400 for every person.
    • 9 million km²of the world’s soil is covered by moss. That is about the area of China.
    • About halfof the CO₂ people give off each year is taken up again by land plants and the sea. The rest stays in the air.
    • More than 10 timesour fossil emissions pass through land plants every year. Nearly all of it is breathed back out. Nature’s account balances. Ours is the part that does not.
    • 1 gram of soilcan hold more bacteria than there are people on Earth.
    How these numbers were worked out
    • Size of the frame. No ruler was in the shot. The saxifrage leaves are taken as 3 cm across, which makes the frame about 57 by 76 cm.
    • Living cover. About a third of the frame, counted from the colour of the photograph. Water covers a little under a fifth.
    • Moss and plants. Net uptake of 2 micromoles of CO₂ per square metre per second in good light and 0.5 given off at night. Yearly growth of 100 g of carbon per square metre, a middling figure for a wet moss carpet.
    • Water. 3 micromoles per square metre per second given off, at the low end of what headwater streams average (Raymond and others, Nature, 2013).
    • Rock. Silicate weathering taking up about 5 g of CO₂ per square metre a year.
    • Making the page. Nobody metered it. Each stage is estimated from the amount of text the models read and wrote (a few million tokens for the first page, several times that for the dives, where six models worked in parallel) and public estimates of data-centre energy per query (Google, 2025; Epoch AI, 2025). Each figure could be three times lower or higher.
    • Carbon per unit of electricity. 470 g of CO₂ per kWh, close to the world average for 2024 (Ember). Your own grid may be far cleaner or dirtier.
    • In proportion. A petrol car at 170 g of CO₂ per km. World fossil emissions of 4.7 tonnes of CO₂ per person a year. A mature tree taking in 20 kg a year, a common round figure; real trees vary several-fold. Water heated from 15 °C in a kettle that wastes a tenth of its power.
    • The living world. Trees: Crowther and others, Nature, 2015. Moss cover: Eldridge and others, Nature Geoscience, 2023. Uptake by land and sea, and the flow through land plants (about 120 billion tonnes of carbon a year against about 10 from fossil fuels): the Global Carbon Budget.
    • Your visit. Sending the page to you once costs less than a hundredth of a watt-hour.

    Plainly

    What is real and what is not

    The picture is the photograph itself. Nothing in it was redrawn. A small program bends it a little, many times a second: the water slides and catches the light, the saxifrage leaves stir, and the brightness drifts as if branches were moving overhead. That movement is invented. It is the kind of thing that happens there, and none of it was recorded.

    The photograph arrived without its location, so the living things are named as far as a picture alone allows. The moss is a cushion moss of some kind. The braided sprays are probably a spikemoss. The round-leaved plant is a saxifrage: in the Alps or Pyrenees it would most likely be Saxifraga rotundifolia, on Madeira S. maderensis, on the Pacific coast of North America S. mertensiana. Knowing where it was taken would settle it.

    The photograph is sharp down to about a millimetre. Past that, each dive hands over to real photographs and micrographs of the same kinds of thing, taken by other people in other places and shared under open licences. Each is credited on screen as you pass it, and all are listed below. They show what is true of a place like this one. They are not this place, and where a stand-in was used (a maize leaf for the pores, a daisy for the pollen grain, a lily for the root hair) the caption says so.

    At the bottom of every dive is a simulation. These are drawn by a program from what is known, and are labelled as simulations. They are meant to be true to how the thing works, and are not a recording of it.
