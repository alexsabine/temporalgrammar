The rhythm

Gold: every flash in the clearing over the last minute. Right: how long each firefly waits between one burst and its next, for the fireflies here as they run, and for one firefly alone, from a run of the same rules.

What is known

Photinus carolinus is a firefly of the southern Appalachians. For about two weeks in late May and June the males fly low over the forest floor and flash in bursts of several flashes about half a second apart. Then they go dark for several seconds. Where there are many of them, the bursts come together and the whole wood flashes and goes dark at once (Copeland and Moiseff 1995). The females answer from the ground, and they answer better when the males around them are in step (Moiseff and Copeland 2010).

Filming swarms in three dimensions showed that synchrony appears only once the fireflies are dense enough, and that a burst often starts in one place and spreads (Sarfati and colleagues 2020, 2021). A firefly kept on its own in a tent flashes at irregular, widely spread intervals. In a swarm the interval becomes regular, and close to the shortest that a lone firefly ever waits (Sarfati and colleagues 2023).

The rule used here

After a burst, each firefly needs a spell of darkness to recover, about nine seconds. A flash seen while it is recovering starts the spell again. Once recovered it may begin a burst on its own at any moment, but rarely: on average after 26 seconds. If it sees a flash first, it joins on the next beat. Within a burst, a flash seen nudges its next flash into step. The flash itself and the recovery follow the shape of Sarfati and colleagues’ model (2023). The nudge is the pulse coupling studied by Mirollo and Strogatz (1990).

Nobody sets the swarm’s rhythm. In a crowd, someone is always ready, so the wait for the first unprompted burst shrinks to almost nothing, and the interval falls to the recovery time and the length of a burst. In test runs a lone firefly waited 43.6 seconds on average, with a spread (coefficient of variation) of 0.69. A swarm of 300 flashed every 14.4 seconds with a spread of 0.05 to 0.07.

The timing rule here is a count, a threshold and a restart: count the dark, burst when the count is full, start the count again. This is the timing grammar of Alexander’s work on coherence, rupture and regeneration, which is under investigation. It is used here as a way to build a firefly, not as a claim about how fireflies decide.

What to try

Press “Hide them from each other”. Each firefly is now in its own tent. The gold spikes smear into a steady drizzle, and the waits on the right spread out until they match the lone firefly’s. Press it again and watch them find each other.

Take “Fireflies” down to one, then bring it back up slowly. Turn on the white torch and sweep it across the clearing: the fireflies it lights stop flashing. Visitors to Elkmont are asked to cover their torches with red cellophane. The red torch here does nothing to the fireflies.

Turn the sound on. Fireflies are silent. Each flash of the swarm is played as a soft note, rising through the burst, over the sound of a stream and a tree cricket.

Where it falls short

Real swarms do not synchronise when they are sparse. This one does. In test runs, fifteen fireflies in the same clearing joined each other’s bursts about as often as three hundred did, around seven in ten each time. Something that makes a sparse swarm fall apart is missing: perhaps how far a flash can really be seen through the leaves, or how often a recovered firefly starts by itself.

Left out: the females on the ground and their answers; how a male’s flight dips as it flashes; the darkening of the evening and the end of the night’s display; differences between fireflies. Sight is a single falling-off with distance, and a leaf in the way stays in the way for four seconds. The white torch silences every firefly it touches. Real light at night affects fireflies more subtly than that (Owens and Lewis 2018). Every number here was chosen to give a lone firefly widely spread intervals. None was fitted to recordings.
